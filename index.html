City Background

Shikumen emerged in Shanghai in the late 19th century as a hybrid housing typology combining Western terrace houses with traditional Chinese courtyard living. During the twentieth century, they became one of the city's dominant residential forms and once accommodated a large proportion of Shanghai's urban population. Today, only a fraction remain, many of which have undergone different approaches to urban regeneration.

City News

Shanghai's shikumen complex 'walks'
back to original site

In 2025, a 7,500-ton Shikumen complex in Shanghai's Zhangyuan was temporarily relocated and later returned to its original site after underground construction. The project demonstrates that historic buildings can be physically preserved through modern technology, while raising a fundamental question:

Is heritage conservation preserving the building itself, or the relationship between architecture, place, and everyday life?

What is Worth Preserving?

This project investigates a series of Shikumen redevelopment cases across different periods of Shanghai's urban transformation. By comparing changes in architectural form, everyday life, community relationships, and commercial activities

Using Shikumen as a lens, the project explores how cities selectively preserve and reconstruct
space, and in doing so, continually redefine what is considered worthy of becoming heritage.

Located in the former Taipingqiao Shikumen neighborhood, Xintiandi was redeveloped between the late 1990s and early 2000s as part of Shanghai's large-scale urban renewal strategy. Led by the government in collaboration with private developers, the project preserved the architectural appearance of Shikumen while replacing its original residential functions with commercial, cultural, and tourism programs. It represents a top-down redevelopment model, where historic architecture became a catalyst for urban branding and economic revitalization.
